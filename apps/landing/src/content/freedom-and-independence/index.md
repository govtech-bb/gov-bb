Barbados' path towards freedom and independence was marked by generations of people who challenged slavery, fought for fair treatment and worked to gain a greater say in how the island was governed.

## Resistance and emancipation

Enslaved people resisted slavery in many ways. Some refused orders or escaped. Others planned uprisings, knowing they risked their lives.

On 14 April 1816, a rebellion began at Bayley's plantation in St. Philip. Enslaved men and women from several plantations joined the fight for freedom. Bussa and Nanny Grigg were among those who helped plan it.

The rebellion spread across several parishes. British soldiers and local forces ended it within days. Bussa was killed in the fighting, and many others were put to death afterwards.

The rebellion did not end slavery, but it remains a powerful part of Barbados' long struggle for freedom.

In 1834, slavery was abolished in Barbados. This meant it was ended by law, but freedom did not come at once. Under a system called apprenticeship, many formerly enslaved people were forced to continue working for their former owners, often without pay.

On 1 August 1838, apprenticeship ended and those who had been enslaved finally gained full freedom under the law. This date is remembered as Emancipation Day.

But freedom under the law did not bring equality. Many families still faced poverty, low wages and little access to land.

## The fight for fair pay and the right to vote

The struggle for voting rights had begun before emancipation. In 1831, free men of African descent who owned enough property gained the right to vote. In 1843, Samuel Jackman Prescod became the first person of African descent elected to the House of Assembly. Most people still could not vote.

By the 1930s, Barbados still depended heavily on sugar. As prices fell, many workers struggled to support their families. Calls for better wages, fairer treatment and a greater say in government grew louder.

Clement Payne spoke out against low wages and poor working conditions. In July 1937, the authorities forced him to leave Barbados. His removal caused days of unrest. But the anger ran deeper, shaped by years of poverty and unfair treatment.

Fourteen people lost their lives during the unrest, and many others were injured. The events drew greater attention to the hardships faced by ordinary Barbadians and strengthened calls for better working conditions and political change.

In 1938, the Barbados Progressive League was formed. It later became the Barbados Labour Party, carrying calls for political change forward.

In 1944, women gained the right to vote in Barbados. Like men, they had to own property or earn enough money to qualify.

In 1950, the property and income rules for voting were removed. At the 1951 election, all men and women aged 21 and over could vote. Edna Bourne became the first woman elected to the House of Assembly.

In 1954, Grantley Adams became Barbados' first Premier. It was another step towards the island governing itself.

In 1964, the voting age was lowered from 21 to 18. More young Barbadians could now help choose their government.

With each change, more people gained a voice in their country's future.

## Independence

In 1961, Barbados gained control over its own affairs at home, although Britain still handled matters such as its relations with other countries. Errol Walton Barrow became Premier, the leader of the government.

On 30 November 1966, Barbados became independent after more than 300 years of British rule. It could now govern itself fully. Barrow became the country's first Prime Minister.

The national flag was raised for the first time that day, marking a new chapter in Barbados' history.

## Barbados becomes a republic

On 30 November 2021, 55 years after independence, Barbados became a republic. Dame Sandra Mason became its first President, replacing Queen Elizabeth II as head of state.

Dame Sandra had been Governor-General, the Queen's representative in Barbados. The country kept its parliamentary system, and the Prime Minister continued to lead the government.

## Historic places and records

Barbados' past can still be seen in its streets, buildings and historic places. [Historic Bridgetown and its Garrison](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1376) tell part of the story of British rule, the sugar trade and slavery. In 2011, UNESCO named the area a World Heritage Site, recognising its importance to people around the world.

The Barbados Museum & Historical Society and the island's archives preserve objects and records that help tell more of Barbados' story. They offer a way to learn about the people who lived here and the lives they led.
