The coat of arms is one of Barbados' national symbols. Its design reflects the island's history and heritage.

At its centre is a gold shield showing a bearded fig tree and two Pride of Barbados flowers. A dolphin and a pelican stand on either side. Above the shield, an arm holds two crossed lengths of sugar cane. The national motto, 'Pride and Industry', appears below.

![The Barbados coat of arms](/images/coat-of-arms.png)

## What the symbols mean

### The bearded fig tree and national flower

Once common in Barbados, the bearded fig tree has long roots that hang like a beard. It is said that Portuguese sailors named the island after these trees. The name means 'the bearded ones'.

The Pride of Barbados blooms for much of the year in shades of red, yellow, orange and pink. The red flower, with petals edged in yellow, is the one recognised as Barbados' [national flower](/about-barbados/national-symbols/national-flower).

### The dolphin and pelican

The dolphin represents Barbados' fishing industry, which has long been part of island life.

The pelican represents Pelican Island, a small island that once lay off the coast of Bridgetown. It was joined to the mainland when the Deep Water Harbour was built.

### The sugar cane and St Andrew's cross

The two crossed lengths of sugar cane represent an industry that shaped Barbados for centuries. Much of the wealth it created came from the forced labour of enslaved Africans on sugar plantations.

The sugar cane also forms an X in the shape of St Andrew's cross. St Andrew, one of Jesus' twelve apostles, is said to have died on such a cross. His feast day falls on 30 November, the date Barbados gained independence in 1966.

Above the shield is a helmet framed by flowing cloth. A twisted band of red and gold cloth, known as a wreath, rests on the helmet. The arm holding the sugar cane rises above it.

### The national motto

The motto reflects pride in Barbados and the hard work, dedication and determination of its people.

## How Barbados got its coat of arms

Before 1966, Barbados used a colonial seal. This was an official symbol of British rule. The seal showed the reigning British king or queen in a shell-shaped chariot drawn across the waves by two seahorses. The image changed with each new ruler. Kings were shown seated, while queens stood.

Neville Connell, who led the Barbados Museum for many years, developed the design. Barbadian artist Hilda Ince brought his early sketch to life through her detailed artwork.

During her visit to Barbados on 14 February 1966, Queen Elizabeth II presented the coat of arms to Sir Grey Massiah. He was President of the Senate at the time.

The coat of arms was granted by royal warrant, an official document issued by a king or queen. This was just months before Barbados gained independence.

## Using the coat of arms

The coat of arms is the official seal of the Government of Barbados. The law controls how it can be used. You need a licence to use or copy it.

If you want to use the coat of arms, contact the Cabinet Office. They can explain how to get a licence. This includes using it on products for sale, in advertising or for an organisation.
