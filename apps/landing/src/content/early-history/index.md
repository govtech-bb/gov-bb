Long before English settlers arrived in 1627, people had made Barbados their home. They cultivated the land, fished its waters and made tools and pottery. The objects they left behind offer glimpses into the lives of the island's earliest inhabitants.

Sugar later transformed Barbados. Large plantations brought wealth to their owners, but that wealth was built on the forced labour and suffering of enslaved Africans. Enslaved people resisted in many ways, from everyday acts of defiance and keeping their cultural traditions alive to organising rebellions in pursuit of freedom. Long after emancipation, generations of Barbadians continued to seek fair treatment, better opportunities and a greater say in how their country was governed.

The people, places, language and traditions of Barbados reflect the island's history. Together, they tell the story of how the nation came to be.

## The island's earliest people

For the island's earliest people, the sea provided food and a way to travel between islands.

At Heywoods in the parish of St. Peter, researchers have found tools made from conch shells and other evidence of early human activity. Some discoveries are believed to be more than 4,000 years old. Researchers are still working to establish when people first settled on the island.

Older historical accounts often describe the island's First Peoples as 'Arawaks' and 'Caribs', as though they belonged to just two distinct groups.

Archaeological evidence tells a longer story. People lived on Barbados during different periods, and later communities left pottery in different styles and evidence of farming. These discoveries show how life on the island changed over thousands of years, although researchers cannot always tell which communities made them or what those people called themselves.

## English settlement and colonial rule

In 1625, English sailors landed on Barbados and declared that the island belonged to the King of England. Two years later, English settlers arrived at what is now Holetown, in St. James.

Barbados became an English colony, meaning the island was under England's control. In 1707, England and Scotland united to form Great Britain, and Barbados became a British colony. British rule continued until Barbados gained independence in 1966.

Historical accounts show that enslaved Africans arrived with the first English settlers in 1627. They had been taken captive and brought to the island against their will. Their arrival marked the beginning of African slavery under English rule in Barbados.

The settlers cleared land and began growing crops such as tobacco and cotton. Some Europeans worked as indentured servants, bound by contracts to work for a set number of years. Enslaved Africans, however, were treated as property and denied their freedom.

In 1639, sixteen landowners were chosen to form Barbados' first House of Assembly. It was one of the earliest in the Americas, but political power remained in the hands of a small group of white landowners. European indentured servants and enslaved Africans had no voice in choosing the island's lawmakers.

As plantations grew, the Assembly would pass laws that strengthened slavery in Barbados and influenced its practice in other English colonies.

## Sugar and the growth of slavery

During the 1640s, sugar began to transform Barbados. Small farms gave way to large plantations, where sugar cane was grown and made into sugar for sale overseas.

By 1680, sugar exports from Barbados were worth more than all exports from England's mainland colonies in North America combined. Despite its small size, Barbados had become one of England's most profitable colonies. The sugar trade brought great wealth to plantation owners, merchants and investors on both sides of the Atlantic. This wealth was built on the forced labour of enslaved Africans.

As sugar plantations grew, thousands of Africans were taken from their homes, forced onto ships and carried across the Atlantic. Many were separated from their families and sold to plantation owners in Barbados.

They were forced to cut sugar cane and work in the mills, where they endured harsh conditions. Beatings and other cruel punishments were used to control them. They had no freedom to leave or decide how they would live.

The change was swift. Within a few decades, the number of enslaved people on the island had grown to tens of thousands.

### The Barbados Slave Code of 1661

In 1661, the Barbados House of Assembly passed a law known as the Barbados Slave Code. It was a set of rules designed to control enslaved Africans and protect the power of plantation owners.

The law treated enslaved people as property, denied them basic freedoms and allowed cruel punishments. It controlled where they could go, how they were treated and what happened if they resisted their enslavers.

Its influence reached far beyond Barbados. The Slave Code became a model for laws in other English colonies, including Jamaica and Carolina in North America. Barbados had helped shape not only the growth of plantation slavery, but also the laws used to enforce it across the Atlantic.

Africans and their descendants also helped shape Barbados through their knowledge, skills, culture and resistance to slavery.
